#03 Principles Week 3

Why week one feels like nothing

Last week: progressive overload. This week: why it takes patience.

The ISSA cert teaches the General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS) — how your body responds to training stress in three stages:

  • Alarm (first 2–3 weeks): fatigue, weakness, soreness. The early strength you gain here is mostly your nervous system learning the movement — not muscle yet.
  • Resistance (around 4–6 weeks): the real changes arrive — muscle size and strength.
  • Exhaustion: what happens if the stress never lets up and you never recover. That's overtraining.

Here's the part worth tattooing on your brain: after a hard enough session, fitness actually dips for a bit — then your body supercompensates, rebuilding to a higher level than before. That rebound is the whole point. But it lives on the far side of recovery, and it runs on weeks, not days.

Do this week: Don't judge a new program by how you feel on day four. Give any change a full 4–6 weeks before you decide it's working.

#12 Principles Week 12

Use it or lose it (literally)

The ISSA principle of reversibility is the one nobody wants to hear: you lose the effects of training after you stop. The cert calls the slide detraining, and it sets in after about two weeks off.

This is why the person who trains at 70% every single week quietly buries the one who goes 100% for ten days and then vanishes. Adaptation is a tenant, not an owner. Stop paying rent and it moves out.

It's also good news. It means the bar to hold your progress is low — far lower than the bar to build it. A couple of short, honest sessions a week is enough to keep what you've earned, even in a busy stretch.

Do this week: Look at your next 7 days and protect two non-negotiable sessions — even 25 minutes counts. The goal isn't a perfect week. It's not handing back what you've already built.

To whom much is given, much is required — including showing up when it's inconvenient.

#21 Principles Week 21

The number that runs your progress

You can't manage what you don't measure. The ISSA cert gives you the number that matters most: training volume.

Volume is the total work you do, and the formula is simple:

Sets × Reps × Load.

Three sets of 10 reps at 100 lbs is 3,000 lbs of volume. Bump to three sets of 12 and you're at 3,600 — more work, more stimulus, without touching the weight. This is what progressive overload looks like in actual numbers, and it's why "I did chest today" tells you nothing while "I beat last week's volume" tells you everything.

There's a sibling number too: density — the same volume done in less time. Cut your rest, finish the same work faster, and you've overloaded a different way.

Do this week: For one exercise, do the math. Sets × reps × load, this week. Write it down. Next week, make that number go up — by adding a rep, a set, a little weight, or finishing the same work with less rest.

#30 Principles Week 30

The one rule that builds everything

Welcome to Brains & Gains. Every Thursday I'll hand you one idea from the ISSA certification — small enough to read in a minute, useful enough to keep. We start with the principle the whole thing is built on.

Progressive overload. Straight from the cert: for fitness to progress, the body must be forced to adapt to a stress greater than what it normally encounters. To build a stronger biceps, you have to lift a resistance it isn't used to. Lift the same weight for the same reps every week and you've given your body no reason to change — it already handles that. That's why people stall while still working hard. They're repeating workouts, not progressing them.

The key word is incremental. Small, steady increases — not heroics.

Do this week: Pick one exercise. Write down what you do — weight, sets, reps. Next week, beat it by the smallest measurable amount: one rep, five pounds, one more set.

Do this week: Focus on including calcium-rich foods in your diet, like dairy products, leafy greens, or fortified alternatives.

#48 Principles Week 48

Why his program won't work for you

Two ISSA principles, one lesson: stop copying.

Individual differences — there's no single way to train every person, because every person is unique. Age, history, recovery, schedule, injuries, starting point. The program that transformed your friend was built for their variables, not yours. Borrow the principles; don't xerox the plan.

Diminishing returns — everyone has a genetic ceiling. The effort that bought huge results in your first year buys smaller ones in your fifth. That's not failure; it's physics. The beginner who adds 50 lbs to a lift in months and the veteran who fights for 5 lbs in a year can be training equally hard.

Put together: compare yourself to last week's you, not to the person whose ceiling, history, and timeline you can't see.

Do this week: Drop one comparison. Unfollow the account, or just stop measuring against that one person. Replace it with your own logbook — what you lifted last week versus this week.

#50 Recovery & Adaptation Week 50

How bones adapt to stress

Bones are living tissues that respond to the demands placed on them. Wolff’s Law states that bone will adapt to the loads it experiences, thickening under stress or thinning when inactive. This means that if you train consistently and apply the right loads, your bones will strengthen. Conversely, if you are sedentary, your bones can weaken over time due to resorption.

Do this week: Include weight-bearing exercises in your routine, such as squats or lunges, to help stimulate bone deposition.

#51 Training Foundations Week 51

Breathe Better, Train Better

Breathing is more than just a reflex; it’s a foundation for your workouts. Vital capacity is the maximum amount of air you can exhale after taking a full breath in. This measure reflects how well your lungs function, which is critical for delivering oxygen to your muscles during exercise.

When your vital capacity is high, your body can perform more efficiently, allowing you to sustain intensity and recover faster. Poor breathing mechanics can limit your performance and even lead to fatigue during workouts.

Do this week: Focus on deep diaphragmatic breathing during your warm-ups. Inhale deeply through your nose, expanding your belly, and exhale fully through your mouth.
